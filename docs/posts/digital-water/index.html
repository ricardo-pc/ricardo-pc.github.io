Digital Water: Forecasting the Water Supply That Later Ran Out

Machine Learning

An LSTM forecasting platform for Monterrey’s water demand and dam capacity, built in 2020. It took 2nd place globally at the Atos IT Challenge, and the shortfall scenario it was designed to flag arrived in 2022.

NoteProject Overview

A forecasting platform for water demand and dam capacity in Monterrey, Mexico, built on LSTM networks trained on records from the city’s water utility. It won 2nd place globally at the Atos IT Challenge 2020, the first Mexican team to place in the competition’s ten-year history.

Two years later the city ran out of water. The system had been built for exactly that scenario and was never deployed.

Team project · Nov 2019 to Jul 2020 · Stack: Python, TensorFlow, LSTM, Heroku

GitHub Repository · Demo Video

The Problem

Monterrey is Mexico’s third-largest metro area and a major industrial hub. Its water system serves more than 5 million people from a network of dams and underground sources. In 2019 that system was managed reactively: utilities responded to shortages once they arrived rather than seeing them coming.

The goal was to give operators a lead time they did not have. If you can forecast daily demand and dam capacity a few weeks out, you can estimate how many days remain before storage crosses a critical threshold, and you can act while options are still cheap.

What We Built

An end-to-end pipeline, developed between November 2019 and July 2020 with data from Servicios de Agua y Drenaje de Monterrey (SADM).

Data engineering. Multi-source time series from SADM, cleaned and aligned so that daily consumption records lined up with dam capacity measurements, with field dictionaries documenting each source.

Models. Two LSTM networks in TensorFlow, one forecasting daily municipal demand and one forecasting available dam capacity. Their outputs fed a “days-to-threshold” estimate: given current trajectory, how long until storage crosses a safe operating floor.

Delivery. An interactive dashboard on Heroku, plus Colab notebooks so the utility could rerun and inspect the models rather than take the numbers on trust.

What the Models Did and Did Not Do

This is worth stating precisely, because the story invites overclaiming.

The models forecast daily demand and capacity on a short horizon. They did not forecast a multi-year meteorological drought, and no daily-horizon LSTM could. What the system produced was a running estimate of how close the network was to its operating floor, and how fast it was closing.

That distinction matters for what came next. The system did not call the 2022 crisis by date. It was built to make exactly that class of shortfall visible early, and the shortfall arrived.

What Happened in 2022

Monterrey ran out of water.

Nuevo León declared a drought emergency on 2 February 2022. Rationing under the Agua para Todos plan began that March, with La Boca dam at 13% and Cerro Prieto at 7%. By June much of the metro area received water roughly six hours a day, and some neighborhoods received none. In mid-July, Cerro Prieto fell below 1% and stopped supplying the city for the first time since it opened in 1984. Heavy rains in late August and early September refilled the dams, and the state’s emergency coordination wound down that September.

The honest summary is that the risk this system was built to surface is the risk that materialized, and nobody was using the system when it did.

Recognition

  • 2nd place globally, Atos IT Challenge 2020, presented to the Atos CEO and Scientific Board
  • First Mexican team to place in the competition’s ten-year history
  • Competed against a field of international university teams

Technologies & Tools

Models: Python, TensorFlow, two LSTM networks for demand and capacity forecasting

Data: official records from Servicios de Agua y Drenaje de Monterrey, covering consumption patterns, dam levels, and seasonal factors

Delivery: Heroku dashboard, Google Colab notebooks, technical documentation

Reflection

The lesson I took from this is not about the modeling. It is that a forecast nobody is looking at has no value, and the gap between a working model and a deployed one is mostly organizational rather than technical. We optimized for a competition deliverable, which is a demo, and a demo is not an operations tool. Getting from one to the other means somebody at the utility owns the number, checks it on a schedule, and has the authority to act on it. We never built that path, and the model sat in a repository while the dams emptied.

The technical work has aged, too. Looking at it five years on, I would redesign the data pipeline, revisit the modeling approach, and take model stability and scalability far more seriously than we did. The notebooks remain in the repository as they were in 2020.
